Use this pathway for cyclical premenstrual symptoms causing distress or functional impairment. Key tasks: confirm pattern, exclude differentials, assess severity/PMDD, then stepwise management.

Step 1 — Does the pattern fit PMS?

Typical pattern Cyclical
  • Symptoms occur in the luteal phase (days before menses), improve within a few days after onset of menses, and there is a symptom-free interval after menstruation.
  • Common symptoms: irritability, mood lability, anxiety, low mood, fatigue, sleep/appetite change, breast tenderness, bloating, headache, pelvic discomfort.
  • Consider a daily symptom diary for at least 2 cycles to confirm pattern and severity.
Question: Which timing best supports PMS?
Best answer: A Luteal phase
  • Symptoms occur premenstrually and ease shortly after menses begins, with a symptom-free interval post-menses.
  • Use a daily diary for ≥2 cycles to confirm diagnosis and track response.

Step 2 — Severity, PMDD screen, and safety

Assess impact Safety
  • Ask about: functional impairment (work/school/relationships), predominant symptoms (mood vs physical), and baseline mood disorders.
  • PMDD suspicion: severe mood symptoms (e.g., marked irritability, mood swings, anxiety, low mood) causing significant impairment, cyclical pattern.
  • Safety: screen for suicidal ideation/self-harm, domestic violence, substance use where relevant.
  • Consider differentials: major depression/anxiety (non-cyclical), thyroid issues, perimenopause, medication/substance effects.
Question: What is the most important escalation issue to check in severe premenstrual mood symptoms?
Best answer: B PMDD can be severe
  • Always assess safety in severe mood symptoms (SI/self-harm).
  • PMDD features significant cyclical mood symptoms with impairment; consider mental health plan and/or specialist input when severe.

Branch — Severe PMS / PMDD / safety concerns

Prioritise safety Mental health support

Immediate priorities

  • Risk assessment (SI/self-harm), crisis plan if needed
  • Consider MH treatment plan and psychological therapy
  • Rule out non-cyclical mood disorder; confirm cyclical pattern with diary
  • Discuss medication options (SSRI strategy often first-line for PMDD)

Escalation

  • Consider psychiatrist involvement if severe, complex, or unsafe
  • Consider gynaecology input if refractory despite adequate trials
  • Urgent services if imminent risk

Step 3 — First-line non-pharmacological management

Start here 2-cycle diary
  • Education + validation: symptoms are real; aim to restore function.
  • Symptom tracking (daily diary) for ≥2 cycles (also helps show response).
  • Exercise (regular aerobic) and sleep optimisation.
  • Stress management (mindfulness/CBT strategies; consider referral).
  • Limit alcohol; reduce caffeine if it worsens anxiety/insomnia.
Question: Best first-line starting package for most mild–moderate PMS?
Best answer: A Foundations
  • Education + symptom tracking + lifestyle supports underpin all treatment tiers.

Step 4 — Symptom-targeted pharmacological options

SSRIs CHC Tailor
  • Predominant mood symptoms / PMDD: SSRI strategy is often highly effective (continuous or luteal-phase dosing depending on presentation and preference).
  • Contraception desired / hormonal symptoms: consider combined hormonal contraception (cyclic or continuous). Some patients respond better to continuous regimens.
  • Physical symptoms: analgesia for pain, lifestyle measures for bloating, headache management as indicated.
  • Avoid routine benzodiazepines; reserve short courses only in exceptional cases with clear rationale and monitoring.
Question: Most evidence-based medication class for severe cyclical mood symptoms (PMDD-type)?
Best answer: A Monitor
  • SSRIs are commonly first-line for PMDD-type symptoms; dosing strategy can be tailored (continuous vs luteal).
  • Review response after 2–3 cycles and monitor adverse effects and safety.

Step 5 — Adjuncts, shared decision-making, and review

Adjuncts Reassess
  • Use shared decision-making: symptom pattern, contraception goals, adverse effects tolerance, comorbid anxiety/depression.
  • Consider referral for psychological therapy (CBT skills can help mood and coping).
  • Some patients ask about supplements (variable evidence); prioritise proven measures first.
  • Review after 2–3 cycles (diary helps objectively show change).
Question: Best way to confirm diagnosis and measure response?
Best answer: A Objective tracking
  • A diary clarifies cyclicity, severity, triggers, and response to interventions.

Step 6 — Escalation, referral, and documentation tools

Review 2–3 cycles Escalate if unsafe/refractory

If improving

  • Continue plan; reinforce diary and lifestyle foundations
  • Optimise sleep, exercise, stress strategies
  • Plan follow-up around worst week if needed

If not improving

  • Confirm cyclicity (diary) and reconsider differentials/comorbidities
  • Consider mental health plan/psychology; medication strategy adjustments
  • Consider gynaecology input if complex/refractory hormonal symptoms
  • Escalate urgently if safety concerns

Patient handout — Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)

Copy/paste into a message or print.

What it is

  • PMS causes emotional and physical symptoms in the days before a period, which improve soon after bleeding starts.

What helps

  • Track symptoms daily for 2 cycles (helps confirm pattern and shows what works).
  • Regular exercise and good sleep.
  • Stress management (mindfulness/CBT skills).
  • If symptoms are significant, your GP may discuss medication options (for mood symptoms, hormonal options, and symptom relief).

When to seek urgent help

  • If you feel unsafe, have thoughts of self-harm, or your mood becomes severely worse.
