Start

Erectile dysfunction can be a marker of cardiometabolic disease and medication effects, as well as psychosexual factors. This pathway supports structured GP assessment and safe stepwise management.

Step 1 — Clarify the problem (history essentials)

Define Organic vs psychogenic clues
  • Onset/duration: sudden vs gradual; intermittent vs persistent.
  • Situational vs consistent; partner-specific vs all settings.
  • Morning/nocturnal erections present? (often suggests preserved physiology).
  • Erection quality: achieving vs maintaining; rigidity adequate for penetration?
  • Libido changes, orgasm/ejaculation issues, penile pain/curvature (Peyronie features).
  • Cardiometabolic risks: smoking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, obesity, sleep apnoea.
  • Psychosocial: stress, anxiety, depression, relationship factors, performance anxiety, pornography use (if relevant).
  • Medication/substances: antidepressants, antihypertensives, finasteride, opioids, alcohol, recreational drugs.
  • Fertility intentions and STI risk where relevant.
Question: Which feature is more suggestive of psychogenic ED?
Best answer: A
  • Situational ED with preserved nocturnal/morning erections often suggests performance anxiety/psychogenic component.

Step 2 — Red flags + cardiovascular risk (don’t miss)

Urgent/early escalation CV screening

Red flags / urgent considerations

  • Exertional chest pain, unstable angina symptoms, recent MI/stroke, decompensated heart failure.
  • Syncope/presyncope, significant exertional dyspnoea.
  • Priapism risk context or prolonged erection symptoms.
  • Severe depression, suicidal ideation, intimate partner violence (as clinically relevant).
  • Suspected androgen deprivation / pituitary symptoms (severe headaches, visual changes).

CV risk prompts (ED can precede CVD)

  • Assess BP, diabetes status, lipids, smoking, obesity, exercise tolerance.
  • Ask about symptoms of coronary disease; consider overall fitness for sexual activity.
  • Address reversible risks (weight, activity, sleep apnoea, alcohol/smoking).
Smart route: What best fits today?
Question: What is the key medication contraindication to PDE5 inhibitors?
Best answer: A
  • PDE5 inhibitors + nitrates can cause dangerous hypotension. Ensure patient not using nitrates (regular or PRN).

Step 3 — Examination + targeted investigations

Targeted Look for endocrine/vascular

Examination

  • Vitals: BP, BMI/waist.
  • Cardio/vascular: pulses, signs of vascular disease.
  • Genital: penile plaques/curvature, meatal issues, testicular size, varicocele if relevant.
  • Endocrine: gynaecomastia, body hair, muscle mass (hypogonadism clues).
  • Neuro: peripheral neuropathy signs (e.g., diabetes).

Common baseline tests (tailor)

  • Fasting glucose or HbA1c.
  • Fasting lipids.
  • Renal function (if indicated) and LFTs (as indicated).
  • Morning total testosterone if low libido or hypogonadism suspected (confirm on repeat if low).
  • Consider TSH if clinical suspicion.
Question: When is a morning testosterone particularly indicated in ED workup?
Best answer: A
  • Testosterone testing is most useful when symptoms/signs of hypogonadism are present.

Step 4 — First-line management (for most stable patients)

Lifestyle + risk factor optimisation Address causes
  • Explain ED as common and often multifactorial (vascular + psychological + medication + endocrine).
  • Optimise cardiometabolic health: exercise, weight, diabetes/BP/lipids, smoking cessation, alcohol moderation, sleep apnoea screening if indicated.
  • Review medications and consider alternatives if likely contributors.
  • Consider psychosexual counselling when anxiety/relationship factors present.
Question: What is the most useful foundational intervention for ED with vascular risk factors?
Best answer: A
  • ED may be an early marker of vascular disease—risk factor treatment improves sexual and cardiovascular outcomes.

Step 5 — Pharmacotherapy (PDE5 inhibitors) + safety

PDE5 inhibitors Check contraindications

Before prescribing

  • Confirm no nitrates (regular or PRN).
  • Assess cardiovascular stability and exercise tolerance.
  • Check interactions/cautions: alpha-blockers, significant hypotension, severe liver disease, advanced renal disease (dose adjustments may be needed).
  • Discuss expectations: requires sexual stimulation; trial on several occasions; avoid heavy alcohol.

Practical counselling points

  • Plan “first trial” in low-pressure setting.
  • Common adverse effects: headache, flushing, dyspepsia, nasal congestion.
  • Seek urgent care for chest pain (do NOT use nitrates if PDE5 used recently), or prolonged painful erection.
  • If inadequate response: check correct use, timing, dose, and consider alternate agent or referral.
Smart route: What’s the likely dominant driver?
Question: Best next step if PDE5 inhibitor “doesn’t work” after 1 attempt?
Best answer: A
  • Most “failures” are due to timing/dose/stimulation, anxiety, alcohol, or untreated comorbidities.

Step 6 — Review, escalation, referrals

Review 4–12 weeks Escalate if refractory/complex

If improving

  • Continue lifestyle + risk optimisation.
  • Reinforce correct PDE5 use and realistic expectations.
  • Consider tapering performance anxiety through graded exposure + counselling.

If not improving

  • Check adherence/technique; repeat trials.
  • Reassess diagnosis (vascular, endocrine, neuro, medication, psychosexual).
  • Consider referral: urology/men’s health; consider psychosexual therapy.
  • Consider second-line options via specialist: vacuum devices, intraurethral/intracavernosal therapy, penile prosthesis.

Always consider

  • Fertility goals and contraception/STI risk.
  • Depression/anxiety screening and relationship safety where relevant.
  • Document counselling, contraindications check, and safety-netting.

Branch — Red flags / cardiovascular instability

Do not proceed to PDE5 until stable Assess/Refer
  • Prioritise assessment of chest pain/unstable symptoms and overall cardiovascular stability.
  • Arrange urgent evaluation if concerning symptoms (local protocols/ED as appropriate).
  • Once stable: ED workup and risk factor management can proceed.
  • Remember: nitrates and PDE5 inhibitors must not be combined.

Branch — Psychogenic / performance anxiety likely

Common Treat anxiety + relationship
  • Normalise and reframe: anxiety can interrupt arousal and erectile response.
  • Address stress, depression, relationship conflict; consider brief validated screening tools if appropriate.
  • Suggest graded “low pressure” intimacy goals; consider referral to counselling/psychosexual therapy.
  • PDE5 inhibitors may still help by restoring confidence if no contraindications.

Branch — Medication/Substance contributors

Review + rationalise Shared decision

Common contributors (examples)

  • SSRIs/SNRIs and other psychotropics
  • Some antihypertensives (not all; consider alternatives if appropriate)
  • Opioids, finasteride
  • Alcohol excess, cannabis/other drugs

Approach

  • Review indication, dose, timing, and alternatives.
  • Do not cease essential meds abruptly; coordinate changes safely.
  • Consider staged adjustments and monitor symptoms.

Branch — Investigations set + interpretation tips

Baseline cardio-metabolic Tailor
  • HbA1c / fasting glucose: detect diabetes/prediabetes.
  • Fasting lipids: vascular risk; treat per guidelines.
  • Creatinine/eGFR: kidney disease can contribute; guides dosing.
  • Morning total testosterone if hypogonadism suspected; repeat to confirm if low.
  • TSH if clinically indicated.
  • Consider targeted tests based on symptoms (e.g., prolactin if pituitary concern, PSA per shared decision and risk profile).

Branch — Possible hypogonadism (low libido ± symptoms)

Test properly Avoid casual TRT
  • Order morning total testosterone (fasting preferred). If low, repeat to confirm.
  • If confirmed low: consider LH/FSH and other targeted tests as clinically indicated to distinguish primary vs secondary causes.
  • Assess contraindications and fertility intentions before any testosterone therapy consideration.
  • Consider endocrinology/men’s health referral for complex cases or uncertain aetiology.
  • PDE5 inhibitors can still be used for ED symptoms if safe/appropriate, but address underlying cause.

Patient handout — Erectile Dysfunction (ED)

One-page, print-friendly summary.

What ED is

  • Difficulty getting or keeping an erection firm enough for sex. It’s common and often treatable.

Why it happens

  • Blood vessel health (blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, smoking) is a common factor.
  • Stress/anxiety, mood, relationship issues, and some medicines can contribute.
  • Low testosterone is less common but can be relevant when libido is low.

What helps

  • Regular exercise, weight management, better sleep, reducing alcohol, stopping smoking.
  • Managing diabetes, blood pressure, and cholesterol.
  • Reviewing medications with your GP.
  • Tablets such as PDE5 inhibitors may help if safe for you (not used with nitrates).
  • Counselling can help when anxiety or relationship stress is part of the problem.

When to seek urgent help

  • Chest pain or severe shortness of breath with activity or sex.
  • A painful erection lasting longer than a few hours.
  • Severe low mood or feeling unsafe.
