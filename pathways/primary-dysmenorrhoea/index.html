Start

Use this pathway when the presentation is consistent with primary dysmenorrhoea (menstrual pain without pelvic pathology).

Step 1 — Is it likely primary dysmenorrhoea?

Before you reveal guidance: Which features best fit primary dysmenorrhoea?
Best answer: A Pattern
  • Primary dysmenorrhoea: cyclic crampy lower abdominal pain, starts just before/at menses, peaks day 1–2.
  • Often onset within 6–12 months of menarche; may include nausea, diarrhoea, fatigue.

Step 2 — Screen for red flags (secondary dysmenorrhoea)

Decision: Which of the following should trigger investigation/referral?
Best answer: B Consider endometriosis
  • Red flags: onset after 25, progressive pain, non-cyclic pain, dyspareunia, heavy/irregular bleeding, infertility, pelvic mass, systemic symptoms, poor response to NSAIDs + hormonal therapy.
  • If present: consider pelvic exam (as appropriate), pelvic US, STI screen if indicated, and refer to gynaecology when suspicion high or symptoms refractory.

Branch — Suspected secondary dysmenorrhoea / endometriosis

Escalate Investigate

History pointers

  • Dyspareunia (deep), dyschezia, cyclical bowel/bladder symptoms
  • Intermenstrual pain, chronic pelvic pain
  • Subfertility
  • Heavy/irregular bleeding
  • Family history of endometriosis

GP actions

  • Pregnancy test if relevant
  • Pelvic exam if appropriate and consented
  • Pelvic ultrasound (note: may be normal in endometriosis)
  • Trial therapy: NSAIDs + hormonal suppression (often continuous CHC)
  • Refer gynaecology if refractory / significant impact / red flags
  • Urgent assessment if severe acute pelvic pain, peritonism, fever, heavy bleeding, suspected torsion/ectopic

Step 3 — First-line non-pharmacological options

Choose your initial plan:
Best answer: A Adjuncts help
  • Heat therapy: heat pack/hot water bottle to lower abdomen/back.
  • Exercise: regular aerobic activity (ongoing).
  • Relaxation/yoga: breathing/mindfulness; address stress and sleep.
  • TENS can be considered as adjunct or if medication-avoidant.
  • Discuss expectations: aim for function, not “zero pain”.

Step 4 — Pharmacological first-line

Decision: What is the most effective medication strategy?
Best answer: A Check contraindications

Exam Mode hides examples until revealed (no fixed doses included here).

  • NSAIDs are first-line (prostaglandin-mediated pain). Use with food; avoid if renal disease, active PUD, significant GI bleed risk, NSAID allergy, etc.
  • Options commonly used: ibuprofen, naproxen, mefenamic acid (choose based on tolerance/contraindications).
  • Paracetamol may help if NSAIDs contraindicated, but generally less effective.
  • Routine opioids are not recommended for primary dysmenorrhoea.

Step 5 — Hormonal therapy (if NSAIDs inadequate or contraception desired)

Pick best next step if pain persists despite well-timed NSAIDs:
Best answer: A Suppress ovulation
  • CHC (pill/patch/ring) reduces ovulation and prostaglandins; continuous regimens can be especially helpful.
  • Progestogen-only options can be considered if CHC contraindicated.
  • LNG-IUD is highly effective if contraception desired and/or heavy bleeding coexists.
  • Reassess after 2–3 cycles and screen again for red flags if ongoing impact.

Step 6 — Review, investigations, escalation

Review after 2–3 cycles Escalate if refractory

If improving

  • Continue plan; optimise timing of NSAIDs and adherence
  • Encourage ongoing exercise + heat + stress/sleep optimisation
  • Provide school/work plan for severe days

If not improving

  • Re-check red flags; consider endometriosis
  • Pelvic exam if appropriate + consent
  • Pelvic ultrasound if indicated (may be normal in endometriosis)
  • Consider referral to gynaecology if persistent functional impairment
  • Urgent assessment if severe acute pain + peritonism/fever/heavy bleeding

Adolescent counselling add-on

Normalise Safeguarding
  • Normalise: period pain is common, but missing school/work is not “something you must endure”.
  • Explain mechanism: prostaglandin-driven uterine contractions.
  • Plan: start treatment early (heat + timed NSAIDs), keep a symptom diary.
  • Ask privately where appropriate: sexual activity, pregnancy risk, STI symptoms, coercion/safety.
  • Discuss contraception respectfully if relevant; reassure confidentiality within legal limits.
  • Safety net: new severe pain, fainting, fever, heavy bleeding, persistent pain between periods.

Patient handout — Period pain (primary dysmenorrhoea)

You can copy/paste this into a message, print, or add to Best Practice patient resources.

What it is

  • Common crampy pain caused by natural “period chemicals” (prostaglandins) making the uterus contract.

What helps most

  • Heat pack to lower tummy/back.
  • Regular exercise (even light walking).
  • Anti-inflammatory pain relief (NSAIDs) started early (before or at the very start of the period), taken with food if safe for you.

When to come back urgently

  • Severe sudden pain, fever, fainting, or feeling very unwell
  • Very heavy bleeding
  • Pain between periods, pain with sex, or symptoms getting worse over time
